SECTION 1: PROBATE PROCESS & JURISDICTION
================================================================================

Q1: What is probate and what is its fundamental legal purpose?
A: Probate is the judicial process by which a decedent's estate is administered, debts are paid, and assets are distributed to beneficiaries or heirs. Its fundamental purposes include: (1) providing legal authority to transfer title of the decedent's assets; (2) ensuring orderly payment of creditors' claims; (3) resolving disputes regarding the estate; (4) providing judicial supervision to protect beneficiaries and creditors; and (5) establishing a clear chain of title for property transfers.

Q2: Which assets typically pass through probate and which do not?
A: Assets passing through probate generally include: solely-owned real property, bank accounts without beneficiary designations, personal property titled in the decedent's name alone, and assets owned as tenants in common. Non-probate assets include: property held in joint tenancy with right of survivorship, assets with valid beneficiary designations (life insurance, retirement accounts, POD/TOD accounts), property held in revocable or irrevocable trusts, and property transferred via community property with right of survivorship.

Q3: What determines jurisdiction for probate proceedings?
A: Primary jurisdiction (domiciliary probate) lies in the state and county where the decedent was domiciled at death. Domicile requires both physical presence and intent to remain indefinitely. Ancillary probate may be required in other states where the decedent owned real property. For venue within a state, the county of domicile typically controls, though some states allow filing where property is located if no domiciliary proceeding exists.

Q4: What are the different types of probate administration?
A: Common types include: (1) Formal/supervised administration - full court supervision with hearings and ongoing court involvement; (2) Informal/unsupervised administration - minimal court oversight after initial appointment; (3) Summary/small estate administration - expedited process for estates below statutory thresholds (varies by state, typically $50,000-$184,500); (4) Affidavit procedures - for very small estates allowing transfer without formal proceedings; (5) Muniment of title - in some states (e.g., Texas), allows probate of will without full administration when no debts exist.

Q5: What is the typical timeline for probate administration?
A: Timeline varies significantly by jurisdiction and estate complexity. Simple estates may close in 6-9 months, while complex estates can take 2-3 years or longer. Statutory minimum periods often apply: creditor claim periods (typically 3-6 months from notice), minimum distribution waiting periods, and tax clearance requirements. Contested matters, litigation, or complex asset liquidation can extend timelines indefinitely.

Q6: What are the stages of formal probate proceedings?
A: Typical stages include: (1) Filing petition and will (if any) with appropriate court; (2) Notice to heirs, beneficiaries, and creditors; (3) Appointment of personal representative and issuance of letters; (4) Inventory and appraisal of assets; (5) Payment of debts, expenses, and taxes; (6) Filing of accountings (interim and final); (7) Distribution of remaining assets; (8) Final discharge of personal representative and estate closure.

Q7: What is the difference between testate and intestate proceedings?
A: Testate proceedings occur when a valid will exists. The court admits the will to probate, and distribution follows the will's terms (subject to forced heirship rules and creditor claims). Intestate proceedings occur when no valid will exists, and distribution follows the state's intestacy statutes. Intestate proceedings may also occur when a will is declared invalid or covers only partial disposition of assets.

Q8: What constitutional due process requirements apply to probate proceedings?
A: Due process requires: (1) Notice to all interested parties - heirs, beneficiaries, creditors, and others with potential claims; (2) Opportunity to be heard before adverse determinations; (3) Impartial tribunal; (4) Notice must be reasonably calculated to reach interested parties (personal service preferred, publication allowed for unknown parties). Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co. established these constitutional minimums for probate proceedings.

SECTION 2: WILLS & TESTAMENTARY DOCUMENTS
================================================================================

Q9: What are the formal requirements for a valid will?
A: Common law requirements (adopted by most states with variations): (1) Testamentary capacity - testator must be of sound mind, typically 18+ years old; (2) Testamentary intent - intent to create a will disposing of property at death; (3) Written instrument (holographic wills excepted in some states); (4) Signature by testator or by another at testator's direction in testator's presence; (5) Two witnesses (some states require three) who sign in testator's presence; (6) Witnesses must be competent (usually 14+ years); (7) Publication (testator's acknowledgment that document is their will) in some jurisdictions.

Q10: What is testamentary capacity and how is it determined?
A: Testamentary capacity requires the testator understand: (1) The nature and extent of their property; (2) The natural objects of their bounty (family members and others who would naturally inherit); (3) The disposition they are making; (4) How these elements relate to form an orderly plan of distribution. The standard is lower than contractual capacity. Capacity is determined at will execution, not later decline. Evidence includes medical records, witness testimony, complexity of estate, and reasonableness of disposition.

Q11: What is undue influence and what is the legal test?
A: Undue influence occurs when a testator's free will is overcome by another's improper conduct, substituting the influencer's intent for the testator's. Elements typically include: (1) Susceptibility of testator to influence (age, illness, dependency); (2) Opportunity to exert influence (access, control); (3) Improper influence actually exerted; (4) Result showing the effect (disposition contrary to testator's prior intent or natural bounty). Burden usually on contestant, but may shift if confidential relationship plus suspicious circumstances exist. Presumption may arise from confidential relationship + active procurement + benefit to fiduciary.

Q12: What is a holographic will and what jurisdictions recognize them?
A: A holographic will is handwritten and signed by the testator without witnesses. Approximately half of U.S. states recognize holographic wills. Requirements typically include: (1) Material provisions in testator's handwriting; (2) Signature by testator; (3) Evidence of testamentary intent. Some states require the entire will be handwritten; others allow printed portions if material provisions are handwritten. Dates are often required but may not be essential unless necessary to resolve ambiguities or conflicts.

Q13: What is a self-proved will and what is its evidentiary value?
A: A self-proved will includes an affidavit executed by the testator and witnesses before a notary public at will execution, attesting to proper execution formalities. Under the Uniform Probate Code and most state statutes, self-proving affidavits create a rebuttable presumption of proper execution, eliminating the need for witness testimony at probate (though witnesses may still testify if will is contested). The affidavit can be part of the will or attached separately.

Q14: What is the doctrine of integration and how is it applied?
A: Integration determines what pages constitute the will. Papers present at execution and intended by testator to be part of the will are integrated. Factors include: physical connection (stapled, fastened), internal coherence (sequential pagination, continuous text), testator's intent, and witness testimony regarding pages present at execution. Questions arise when pages are loose, unnumbered, or appear altered.

Q15: What is incorporation by reference and what are its requirements?
A: Incorporation by reference allows a will to incorporate an external document without republishing the will. Requirements: (1) Document must exist at will execution; (2) Will must sufficiently describe the document; (3) Will must manifest intent to incorporate it; (4) Document must be identified with certainty. The Uniform Probate Code allows incorporation of lists disposing of tangible personal property even if created after will execution (must be signed and describe items with reasonable certainty).

Q16: What are codicils and how do they interact with the underlying will?
A: A codicil is a testamentary instrument that amends, modifies, or supplements an existing will without replacing it entirely. Codicils must satisfy the same formalities as wills. A codicil republishes the will as of the codicil's date (important for lapsed legacies, after-acquired property, and capacity challenges). Multiple codicils are read together with the will; conflicts are resolved in favor of later instruments. Extensive changes typically warrant a new will rather than codicil.

Q17: What is the doctrine of dependent relative revocation?
A: Dependent relative revocation (DRR) is an equitable doctrine that disregards a revocation when the testator's revocatory act was premised on a mistake of law or fact and the testator would not have revoked but for that mistake. Courts apply DRR when reinstating the revoked will comes closer to testator's intent than intestacy. Common scenarios include revoking a will in anticipation of executing a new will that proves invalid, or destroying a will based on mistaken belief about legal effect.

Q18: What are the methods of will revocation?
A: Wills may be revoked by: (1) Physical act - burning, tearing, canceling, obliterating, or destroying with intent to revoke (by testator or another at testator's direction in presence); (2) Subsequent instrument - express revocation clause or inconsistent provisions; (3) Operation of law - divorce (in most states, revokes provisions for ex-spouse), pretermitted children statutes, elective share statutes; (4) Partial revocation by physical act (recognized in some states for deletions/markings on will). Intent to revoke is essential for all methods.

Q19: What is the plain meaning rule and when do courts depart from it?
A: The plain meaning rule requires courts to give effect to will language according to its ordinary meaning. Extrinsic evidence is generally inadmissible to vary unambiguous terms. Exceptions exist for: (1) Patent ambiguity (uncertainty on face of will); (2) Latent ambiguity (uncertainty arising from applying will to facts); (3) Mistake in inducement or execution (limited relief); (4) Scrivener's error (increasingly recognized). Some jurisdictions have adopted reformation statutes allowing correction for clear and convincing evidence of mistake.

Q20: What is ademption by extinction and how do courts apply it?
A: Ademption by extinction occurs when specifically bequeathed property is not in the estate at death, causing the gift to fail. The identity theory (traditional rule) applies ademption mechanically - if property is gone, gift adeems regardless of intent. The intent theory (modern/UPC approach) examines whether the testator intended ademption, considering reasons for disposition (sale, destruction, condemnation). Exceptions exist for: proceeds traceable to specific property, sale by guardian/conservator, executory contracts, insurance proceeds, and condemnation awards.

SECTION 3: INTESTATE SUCCESSION
================================================================================

Q21: What is intestate succession and when does it apply?
A: Intestate succession is the statutory distribution scheme that applies when a person dies without a valid will, with a will that fails to dispose of all property, or when beneficiaries predecease or disclaim. Each state has intestacy statutes (often based on Uniform Probate Code) prescribing distribution to heirs based on family relationships. Intestacy rules also provide default rules for class gifts and other will construction issues.

Q22: How do most states distribute property to a surviving spouse under intestacy?
A: Distribution varies significantly by state. Common patterns include: (1) Entire estate to spouse if no descendants or all descendants are of the surviving spouse (UPC approach); (2) Fractional share to spouse with remainder to descendants (e.g., 1/2 to spouse, 1/2 to children); (3) Fixed dollar amount plus fraction (UPC: $300,000 plus 3/4 if descendants from both spouses); (4) Different treatment for community vs. separate property (community property states). Modern statutes increasingly favor the surviving spouse, especially in smaller estates or when all descendants are mutual.

Q23: What is the per stirpes distribution method?
A: Per stirpes (also called "by representation" or "by roots") divides the estate into shares at the first generational level, even if members are deceased. Living members take their share; shares of deceased members pass to their descendants. Example: If testator has 3 children (A, B, C), and A predeceases with 2 children, B predeceases with 1 child, and C survives - estate divides in thirds. A's children split A's 1/3 (1/6 each), B's child takes B's 1/3, and C takes 1/3. Division always occurs at the children's generation.

Q24: What is per capita at each generation and how does it differ from per stirpes?
A: Per capita at each generation (modern per capita/UPC approach) pools shares of deceased persons at each generation and divides equally among living members at that generation. Using the same example: A's 1/3 and B's 1/3 are pooled (2/3 total) and divided equally among the 3 grandchildren (2/9 each), while C still takes 1/3. This treats all members of a generation equally regardless of how many siblings their parent had. This is the UPC default and increasingly common.

Q25: Who qualifies as a child for intestacy purposes?
A: Generally includes: (1) Biological children (marital and non-marital if paternity established); (2) Adopted children (full inheritance rights from adoptive parents; typically not from biological parents unless adoption by stepparent); (3) Children born posthumously (if in gestation at death); (4) Assisted reproduction children (if statutory requirements met - often requires consent or posthumous use provisions). Typically excludes: stepchildren (unless adopted), foster children, and children whose parental rights were terminated. Half-blood siblings typically inherit equally with whole-blood siblings under modern statutes.

Q26: What are the requirements for establishing paternity for intestacy purposes?
A: Paternity for inheritance typically established by: (1) Marriage to mother at conception or birth (presumption); (2) Adjudication of paternity during father's lifetime; (3) Father's acknowledgment (paternity affidavit, birth certificate signing); (4) Clear and convincing evidence of paternity (varies by state - may include DNA testing, conduct, declarations). Uniform Probate Code requires clear and convincing evidence. Some states impose time limits for posthumous paternity establishment. Standards differ for inheriting FROM child vs. THROUGH child.

Q27: What is advancement and how is it applied in intestacy?
A: Advancement is a lifetime gift to an heir treated as a prepayment of inheritance, reducing that heir's intestate share. Common law presumed lifetime gifts were advancements; modern statutes (including UPC) require: (1) Written declaration by decedent that gift is an advancement, or (2) Written acknowledgment by heir. Advanced property is valued at time of gift (minority rule) or distribution (UPC rule). If advancement exceeds heir's share, no return required but heir receives nothing additional (hotchpot method).

Q28: What is the 120-hour survival requirement?
A: Under UPC §2-104 and similar state statutes, an heir or beneficiary must survive the decedent by 120 hours (5 days) to inherit. This prevents property from passing through an heir who dies shortly after the decedent, avoiding double administration and ensuring property reaches more remote beneficiaries. Requirement applies unless will provides otherwise. Insufficient evidence of survival order results in each person's property passing as if they survived the other (relevant for simultaneous deaths or unclear timing).

Q29: How do community property states handle intestate succession differently?
A: In community property states (AZ, CA, ID, LA, NV, NM, TX, WA, WI), property classification affects distribution. The surviving spouse generally retains their 1/2 of community property automatically (not via inheritance). The decedent's 1/2 of community property passes under intestacy statutes (often entirely to surviving spouse if no issue, or in shares if issue exist). Separate property distribution follows standard intestacy rules. Quasi-community property (acquired in non-community states) may be treated as community property.

Q30: What happens to property when no heirs can be identified?
A: When no heirs exist under intestacy statutes (typically after exhausting parents, siblings, and more remote relatives to varying degrees), property escheats to the state. Most states cut off inheritance at a certain degree of relationship (e.g., grandparents and their descendants; some states allow more remote relatives). Uniform Probate Code allows inheritance by grandparents and their descendants. The state takes as sovereign, not as heir. Some states dedicate escheated funds to specific purposes (education, indigent care).

SECTION 4: ESTATE ADMINISTRATION & PERSONAL REPRESENTATIVES
================================================================================

Q31: What is a personal representative and what types exist?
A: A personal representative is the court-appointed fiduciary responsible for administering the estate. Types include: (1) Executor/executrix - named in will; (2) Administrator - appointed when no will exists or executor cannot serve; (3) Administrator with will annexed (administrator c.t.a.) - appointed when will exists but no executor named/able to serve; (4) Special administrator - temporary appointment for limited purposes pending final appointment; (5) Public administrator - government official serving when no qualified private party available.

Q32: What are the qualifications and disqualifications for serving as personal representative?
A: General qualifications include: legal age (18+), mental competency, and absence of disqualifying factors. Disqualifications typically include: felony convictions (particularly involving dishonesty), conflicts of interest, unsuitability to perform duties, and non-residency (though many states now allow non-resident personal representatives, sometimes requiring resident agent or bond). Corporate fiduciaries must be authorized to serve (banks, trust companies). Priority for appointment usually follows: named executor, surviving spouse, adult children, parents, siblings, creditors, others.

Q33: What is the fiduciary duty of a personal representative?
A: Personal representatives owe comprehensive fiduciary duties including: (1) Duty of loyalty - act solely in estate's interest, avoid self-dealing and conflicts; (2) Duty of care - act with prudence, skill, and diligence of a reasonably prudent person; (3) Duty of impartiality - treat beneficiaries fairly per their respective interests; (4) Duty to inform and account - provide information and regular accountings; (5) Duty to preserve and protect assets; (6) Duty to segregate estate assets from personal assets. Breach can result in surcharge, removal, and personal liability.

Q34: What powers does a personal representative have?
A: Powers vary by state and governing instrument but generally include: (1) Take possession of estate assets; (2) Manage and invest estate property; (3) Sell, lease, or encumber property (court approval may be required for real property); (4) Continue decedent's business (limited duration, court approval often required); (5) Pay debts, taxes, and expenses; (6) Prosecute and defend claims; (7) Distribute assets; (8) Employ professionals (attorneys, accountants, appraisers). Independent administration statutes grant broader powers without court approval. Will provisions can expand or restrict powers.

Q35: What is the inventory and appraisal requirement?
A: Most states require personal representatives to file an inventory listing all estate assets with fair market values as of date of death. Time limits vary (typically 60-90 days after appointment). Inventory must include: description of each asset, valuation, category (real/personal, probate/non-probate). Professional appraisers may be required for certain assets (real property, business interests, unique items). Inventory provides notice to beneficiaries and basis for accountings. Supplemental inventories may be required for subsequently discovered assets.

Q36: What are the personal representative's duties regarding creditors?
A: Personal representatives must: (1) Publish notice to creditors (typically in local newspaper, statutorily prescribed period); (2) Provide direct notice to known or reasonably ascertainable creditors; (3) Review and evaluate claims; (4) Allow, reject, or negotiate claims; (5) Pay valid claims in statutory priority order; (6) Obtain tax clearances before distributions. Failure to provide proper notice can extend or eliminate statute of limitations for creditor claims. Personal representative may be personally liable for improper rejection of valid claims or distribution before paying creditors.

Q37: What is the priority for payment of claims against an estate?
A: Typical statutory priority (varies by state): (1) Costs of administration (court fees, attorney fees, personal representative fees, appraisal costs); (2) Funeral and burial expenses (sometimes capped); (3) Homestead and family allowances; (4) Federal taxes; (5) State taxes; (6) Medical expenses of last illness (may have time limit, e.g., last 60 days); (7) General unsecured creditors; (8) Specific devises (to extent estate is insolvent). Within classes, creditors are paid pro rata if assets insufficient. Some states give secured creditors priority; others treat them separately as secured by specific property.

Q38: What are exempt property, homestead allowance, and family allowance?
A: These are statutory protections for surviving family: (1) Exempt property - personal property set aside for family (vehicles, household goods, etc.) up to statutory limit (UPC: $15,000), taking priority over creditors except secured creditors in the property; (2) Homestead allowance - monetary allowance to surviving spouse and/or minor children (UPC: $22,500), taking priority over all claims except administration costs; (3) Family allowance - temporary support during administration for spouse and dependent children (reasonable amount based on accustomed standard of living), taking priority over all claims except administration and funeral costs. These exist regardless of will provisions and are in addition to other inheritance.

Q39: What accounting requirements apply to personal representatives?
A: Personal representatives typically must file: (1) Initial inventory (described above); (2) Periodic accountings (annual or as required by court/beneficiaries), showing: opening balance, receipts, disbursements, asset changes, distributions, and closing balance; (3) Final accounting before distribution and discharge. Accountings must detail all transactions, classify receipts and disbursements (principal vs. income), and reconcile balances. Beneficiaries have right to examine accountings and object. Informal accountings may be provided to beneficiaries; formal accountings are filed with court. Detailed records (receipts, statements, documentation) must be maintained.

Q40: When and how is a personal representative entitled to compensation?
A: Personal representatives are entitled to reasonable compensation for services. Determination methods vary: (1) Will provision specifying amount or method; (2) Statutory formula (percentage of estate value, graduated scales common); (3) Reasonable fee based on time, skill, complexity, and results (court approval required). Family members may waive fees. Extraordinary services (litigation, business operation, contested matters) may justify additional compensation beyond statutory percentages. Attorney fees are separate. Corporate fiduciaries typically charge per published fee schedules. Fees are deductible for estate tax purposes.

Q41: Under what circumstances can a personal representative be removed?
A: Grounds for removal include: (1) Mismanagement or waste of assets; (2) Breach of fiduciary duty; (3) Failure to perform duties; (4) Incapacity or illness preventing performance; (5) Conviction of crime; (6) Conflict of interest; (7) Hostility toward beneficiaries interfering with administration; (8) Neglect or unreasonable delay. Removal requires court proceeding initiated by interested party. Standard varies from "good cause" to showing harm to estate. Less drastic remedies (requiring bond, limiting powers, requiring co-fiduciary) may be imposed before removal.

Q42: What is the personal representative's liability for taxes?
A: Personal representatives are responsible for: (1) Filing estate tax returns (federal Form 706 if estate exceeds exemption, state returns as required); (2) Filing decedent's final income tax returns (federal and state); (3) Filing estate/trust income tax returns (Form 1041) for administration period; (4) Paying all taxes owed; (5) Obtaining tax clearances before distribution. Personal representative may be personally liable for taxes to extent of distributed assets if distributions made before paying taxes. IRS discharge from liability can be requested. Statute of limitations generally runs 3 years from filing but can be extended.

Q43: What happens to the estate when the personal representative dies during administration?
A: If personal representative dies during administration: (1) Authority terminates at death; (2) Successor personal representative must be appointed (will may name successors; otherwise court appoints per statutory priority); (3) Estate administration continues with successor assuming all duties; (4) Accounting is required for deceased representative's administration period; (5) If co-representatives exist, surviving co-representative may continue if authorized. The deceased representative's estate is liable for any breaches committed during their administration.

Q44: What is independent administration and how does it differ from supervised administration?
A: Independent administration (available in most states) allows personal representatives to administer estates without ongoing court supervision. Key differences from supervised administration: (1) No court approval required for routine actions (sales, distributions, investments); (2) Fewer required court filings and hearings; (3) Faster, less expensive administration; (4) Greater personal representative discretion; (5) No court review of actions unless interested party objects. Requirements vary: some states require will authorization; others allow by statute or beneficiary consent. Supervised administration provides more oversight and may be required for complex/contested estates or when protection needed.

SECTION 5: TRUSTS & TRUST ADMINISTRATION
================================================================================

Q45: What are the essential elements of a valid trust?
A: A valid private express trust requires: (1) Settlor with capacity to create trust; (2) Present intent to create trust (precatory language insufficient); (3) Identifiable trust property (res/corpus); (4) Ascertainable beneficiaries (except charitable trusts and certain purpose trusts); (5) Trustee (court will appoint if none designated and settlor manifested intent); (6) Valid trust purpose (not illegal or against public policy); (7) Compliance with Statute of Frauds (writing required for real property; oral trusts of personalty generally valid). Merger doctrine voids trust if sole trustee is sole beneficiary with no active duties.

Q46: What is the difference between revocable and irrevocable trusts?
A: Revocable trusts (living trusts) can be amended or revoked by settlor during life, settlor retains control, assets included in taxable estate, creditors can reach assets, and settlor typically serves as initial trustee. Irrevocable trusts cannot be modified or revoked (absent court reformation or beneficiary consent in some states), settlor relinquishes control, assets removed from taxable estate (if properly structured), creditors generally cannot reach assets, and independent trustee typically required. Revocable trusts become irrevocable at settlor's death. Under UTC and modern statutes, trusts are presumed revocable unless expressly made irrevocable.

Q47: What are the trustee's fiduciary duties?
A: Trustees owe comprehensive fiduciary duties: (1) Duty of loyalty - administer trust solely in beneficiaries' interest, no self-dealing, avoid conflicts; (2) Duty of prudence - invest and manage trust property as prudent investor would, considering purposes, terms, distribution requirements, and circumstances (Prudent Investor Rule); (3) Duty of impartiality - balance interests of current and remainder beneficiaries; (4) Duty to inform and account - keep beneficiaries reasonably informed, provide periodic accountings; (5) Duty to preserve trust property and make it productive; (6) Duty to control and protect trust property; (7) Duty to enforce and defend claims; (8) Duty to segregate trust assets; (9) Duty to comply with trust terms and law. Violations lead to surcharge and removal.

Q48: What is the Uniform Prudent Investor Act and how has it changed trust investment standards?
A: UPIA (adopted in most states) modernized investment standards: (1) Total return approach - evaluate performance of entire portfolio, not individual investments; (2) Modern portfolio theory - diversification required, balancing risk and return; (3) Risk/return tradeoff recognized - higher risk acceptable if justified by circumstances and trust purposes; (4) Delegation permitted - trustee may delegate investment decisions to qualified agents if prudent; (5) Impartiality measured by total return, not allocation between income and principal; (6) Circumstances-specific - no prohibited categories of investments; appropriateness depends on portfolio role. This replaced legal list restrictions and speculation rules.

Q49: What is the duty of impartiality and how do trustees satisfy it?
A: Trustees must act impartially when administering trust for multiple beneficiaries with different interests (typically income beneficiaries vs. remaindermen). Traditional approach: invest to provide reasonable current income while preserving principal. Modern approach under Prudent Investor Act: pursue total return strategy with power to adjust between income and principal (unitrust conversion). Uniform Principal and Income Act provides default allocation rules for receipts and disbursements. Trustee may petition court for instruction when balancing competing interests. Modern statutes allow trust terms or court order to convert to unitrust (fixed percentage distribution regardless of income).

Q50: What is a pour-over will and how does it interact with a revocable trust?
A: A pour-over will devises estate assets to a previously established trust (typically revocable living trust). Validity supported by: (1) UTATA (Uniform Testamentary Additions to Trusts Act, adopted in most states) - allows pour-over to trust established before or concurrently with will, even if trust unfunded during life, and trust can be amended after will execution; (2) Incorporation by reference (if trust existed at will execution); (3) Independent legal significance. Pour-over enables unified administration of probate and trust assets, maintains privacy for trust assets (will is public record), and allows single distribution scheme. Assets pour into trust as amended at death, not as it existed at will execution.

Q51: What are Crummey powers and why are they used?
A: Crummey powers (named from Crummey v. Commissioner) are temporary withdrawal rights given to trust beneficiaries, typically used in irrevocable life insurance trusts. Purpose: qualify gifts to trust for annual gift tax exclusion ($18,000/2024), which requires present interest. Mechanism: When contribution made to trust, beneficiaries receive notice and have limited time (typically 30-60 days) to withdraw contribution. Beneficiaries typically don't exercise withdrawal (understanding doing so defeats trust purpose), but mere existence of right qualifies for present interest. Requires: actual notice to beneficiaries, genuine right to withdraw, and sufficient time to exercise.

Q52: What is a Special Needs Trust and what rules govern distributions?
A: A Special Needs Trust (Supplemental Needs Trust) preserves beneficiary's eligibility for needs-based government benefits (SSI, Medicaid) while providing supplemental support. Key features: (1) Trustee has absolute discretion (no ascertainable standard); (2) Distributions for supplemental needs only, not basic support (shelter, food) covered by benefits; (3) Cannot be available to beneficiary or creditors; (4) First-party SNTs (beneficiary's own assets) require payback provision to state Medicaid program at death; third-party SNTs (funded by others) do not. Pooled trusts and d4A trusts have specific requirements. Distribution requests must be carefully evaluated to avoid benefit disqualification.

Q53: What is trust decanting and what are the requirements?
A: Trust decanting allows trustee to distribute assets from one trust to another trust with different terms, effectively modifying the trust. Available in states with decanting statutes (approximately 30 states) or under broad distribution authority. Requirements typically include: (1) Trustee must have absolute or expanded distribution authority; (2) Beneficiaries of new trust must be same as or subset of original beneficiaries; (3) Cannot reduce fixed income/annuity interests; (4) Notice requirements to beneficiaries; (5) Cannot extend perpetuities period; (6) Cannot defeat grantor's tax status or charitable deduction. Used to update administrative provisions, modify distributions, respond to changed circumstances, or correct drafting errors.

Q54: What is the Rule Against Perpetuities and how do modern statutes address it?
A: Common law RAP voids interests that might not vest within lives in being plus 21 years. "No interest is good unless it must vest, if at all, not later than 21 years after some life in being at the creation of the interest." Modern reforms include: (1) Wait-and-see statutes - validity determined by actual events, not possibilities; (2) Uniform Statutory Rule Against Perpetuities (USRAP) - 90-year alternative vesting period; (3) Reformation/cy pres - reform instrument to comply with RAP; (4) Dynasty trust statutes - many states abolished RAP entirely or extended to 365-1,000 years. Delaware, South Dakota, Alaska, and others allow perpetual trusts. Generation-skipping transfer tax (1,000-year exemption period) now more relevant limitation.

Q55: What are the requirements for trust modification or termination?
A: Trust modification/termination may occur through: (1) Trust terms permitting modification/termination; (2) Settlor consent if revocable; (3) Settlor and all beneficiaries consent (traditional rule); (4) Beneficiaries consent if modification not inconsistent with material purpose (UTC approach); (5) Court modification/termination for changed circumstances (equitable deviation), unanticipated circumstances, uneconomical small trust, tax objectives; (6) Nonjudicial settlement agreements (UTC - allows if not inconsistent with material purpose); (7) Decanting (described above). Material purposes preventing premature termination typically include: spendthrift provisions, support provisions, discretionary distributions, and specific age distributions.

Q56: What is a directed trust and how does it allocate fiduciary responsibilities?
A: Directed trusts divide fiduciary responsibilities among multiple parties: (1) Administrative trustee - handles custody, distributions, accountings; (2) Investment advisor/director - makes investment decisions; (3) Distribution advisor/director - directs distributions; (4) Trust protector - power to modify terms, remove/replace trustees. Authorized by statute in most states. Liability allocation: directed party liable for their decisions; following party not liable for actions taken pursuant to direction (absent bad faith/knowledge of breach). Allows settlor to select experts for specific functions, reduces trustee liability, permits family member involvement without full fiduciary responsibility.

Q57: What is a trust protector and what powers do they typically have?
A: A trust protector is a person or entity given specific powers to oversee and modify trust administration. Common powers include: (1) Remove/replace trustee; (2) Modify administrative provisions; (3) Change situs/governing law; (4) Veto distributions; (5) Modify dispositive provisions (within limits); (6) Terminate trust; (7) Direct trust distributions or investments. Not a trustee (no general fiduciary duty to beneficiaries in most states, only duty to act in good faith regarding specific powers). Used in long-term irrevocable trusts to provide flexibility for changed circumstances without court proceedings. Powers must be specifically granted in trust instrument.

Q58: What are the differences between trustee accounting and beneficiary reporting requirements?
A: Trustee accounting requirements include: (1) Formal accountings - comprehensive financial reports following state statutory format (receipts, disbursements, asset changes, distributions) filed with court or provided to beneficiaries at specified intervals; (2) Informal reports - periodic statements of account activity; (3) Annual reporting to beneficiaries (required by UTC - account statement showing receipts, disbursements, assets, liabilities, compensation paid). Beneficiaries have right to: receive accountings, examine supporting documentation, petition for formal accounting, and object to transactions. Formal accountings may be waived by adult competent beneficiaries. Virtual representation statutes allow representation of unborn/minor/incapacitated beneficiaries.

Q59: What is the merger doctrine and when does it apply to trusts?
A: The merger doctrine terminates a trust when legal and equitable title merge in the same person. Occurs when: (1) Sole trustee is sole beneficiary; (2) No active duties remain; (3) No purpose remains to be accomplished. Trust does not merge when: (1) Active duties exist (management, discretionary distributions, support); (2) Multiple beneficiaries exist (even if one is also trustee); (3) Spendthrift provisions; (4) Beneficiary holds present interest only and others hold future interests. Passive trusts (bare legal title with no duties) may merge, eliminating the trust. Relevant to termination, reformation, and determining when trust purposes are accomplished.

Q60: What remedies are available for breach of trust?
A: Remedies for breach of fiduciary duty include: (1) Surcharge - personally liable for loss or depreciation caused by breach, plus lost profits; (2) Disgorgement of profits from self-dealing; (3) Removal as trustee; (4) Denial of compensation; (5) Constructive trust on wrongfully obtained property; (6) Tracing and recovery of trust property; (7) Injunction against threatened breach; (8) Appointment of receiver; (9) Modification of trust terms or powers. Statute of limitations typically runs 3-6 years from final accounting or discovery of breach. Beneficiaries may ratify breach if adults with full knowledge. Exculpatory clauses (limiting liability) are valid if not inserted through abuse of fiduciary relationship.

SECTION 6: CLAIMS AGAINST THE ESTATE
================================================================================

Q61: What is the statute of limitations for creditor claims against an estate?
A: Limitations periods vary significantly by state. Common approaches: (1) Nonclaim statutes - short period (typically 3-6 months) from publication of notice to creditors, regardless of actual notice; (2) Longer period (1-3 years) for creditors receiving actual notice; (3) Some states distinguish known/reasonably ascertainable creditors (requiring actual notice, longer period) from unknown creditors (publication notice sufficient, shorter period). Constitutional due process requires actual notice to known/reasonably ascertainable creditors. Claims filed after deadline are barred absent fraud or concealment. Personal representative's failure to publish notice may extend or eliminate limitations period.

Q62: How are tort claims against the decedent handled in probate?
A: Tort claims survive death and may be asserted against the estate if timely filed. Issues include: (1) Claims based on decedent's actions filed within creditor claim period; (2) State survival statutes determine which claims survive death (generally property torts and personal injury claims survive; some states bar pain and suffering); (3) Wrongful death claims (separate statutory action) generally not estate claims but filed by specific statutory beneficiaries; (4) Estate's liability limited to estate assets (no personal liability of heirs); (5) Insurance proceeds may be available; (6) Some states limit tort claims to prevent estate depletion; (7) Punitive damages may not survive in some states.

Q63: What is the order of priority for paying debts when the estate is insolvent?
A: When estate assets are insufficient to pay all claims, statutory priority controls (described in Q37). Key principles: (1) Administration costs take absolute priority (ensures estate can be administered); (2) Family protections (homestead, allowances) come early; (3) Taxes are high priority; (4) General creditors paid pro rata within their class if assets insufficient; (5) Beneficiaries receive nothing in insolvent estate; (6) Abatement rules determine which specific bequests are reduced first (see Q76). Personal representative must marshal assets, pay in proper order, and obtain court approval for insolvent estates. Improper priority subjects personal representative to surcharge.

Q64: Can a personal representative settle or compromise claims?
A: Personal representatives generally have authority to settle claims with proper exercise of discretion. Considerations: (1) Must act prudently and in estate's best interest; (2) Settlement of substantial claims typically requires court approval; (3) Must provide notice to interested parties; (4) Court evaluates: claim validity, settlement amount reasonableness, estate's ability to prevail, litigation costs; (5) Competing claims among beneficiaries may require court approval; (6) Compromises for less than full value must be justified. Authority may be limited by will or statute. Rejection of claims triggers creditor's burden to file independent action.

Q65: What is the procedure when a creditor's claim is rejected?
A: When personal representative disallows a claim: (1) Written notice of rejection must be provided to claimant; (2) Creditor has limited time (typically 60-90 days) to file independent lawsuit in state court against estate; (3) Failure to timely sue results in claim being forever barred; (4) Suit is tried as ordinary civil proceeding; (5) Personal representative defends on behalf of estate; (6) Judgment against estate becomes allowed claim payable in ordinary course; (7) Some states require probate court approval of judgment before payment. Procedural requirements are strict; failure to follow procedure results in claim bar.

Q66: How are tax liabilities handled as estate claims?
A: Tax claims receive high statutory priority and special treatment: (1) Estate tax (Form 706) is estate liability, due 9 months after death (extensions available); (2) Decedent's final income tax returns due on normal filing date of year following death; (3) Estate/trust income tax (Form 1041) filed annually during administration; (4) Tax claims not subject to creditor claim bar statutes; (5) IRS has extended collection period (up to 10 years); (6) Tax liens attach to estate assets; (7) Personal representative personally liable if distributes assets before paying taxes; (8) Request discharge from liability from IRS/state; (9) Pay tax before distributing or withhold sufficient reserves. Careful tax planning required.

Q67: What happens to secured claims in probate?
A: Secured creditors have rights in specific collateral: (1) Security interest survives death and transfers with property; (2) Two options: (a) foreclose on collateral through secured creditor remedies, or (b) file claim in probate for deficiency (if any); (3) Beneficiary receiving encumbered property generally takes subject to lien unless will provides otherwise; (4) Exoneration doctrine (abolished in many states) - some states required estate to pay off mortgages on specifically devised property; modern rule: specific devisee takes subject to liens unless will directs otherwise; (5) Personal representative may pay secured debt and remove lien if beneficial to estate; (6) Property may be sold free and clear with lien attaching to proceeds (court approval required).

Q68: What is a family allowance and how does it function as a claim against the estate?
A: Family allowance (described in Q38) is statutory support for surviving spouse and dependent children during administration. Key features: (1) Priority over most claims (except administration costs and funeral expenses in many states); (2) Reasonable amount based on family's accustomed standard of living; (3) Typically continues for one year or until final distribution (whichever first); (4) Petition required, court determines amount; (5) Paid from estate assets regardless of will provisions; (6) Separate from and in addition to: homestead allowance, exempt property, and inheritance; (7) May be paid as periodic allowance or lump sum; (8) Protects family from impoverishment during administration delays.

Q69: How are contingent and unliquidated claims handled in probate?
A: Claims that are contingent or not yet reduced to judgment present special issues: (1) Should be filed within creditor claim period despite uncertainty; (2) Personal representative may: (a) pay if amount reasonably certain; (b) petition for court determination; (c) negotiate settlement; (d) await triggering event/litigation outcome; (3) Estate closure may be delayed pending resolution; (4) Personal representative may seek discharge while maintaining reserve for contingent claims; (5) Distribution of estate may proceed with: (a) reserve held for claim, (b) bond or security from beneficiaries for potential liability, (c) court approval of distribution despite pending claim. Examples include pending lawsuits, warranty claims, environmental liabilities.

Q70: What defenses can an estate raise to creditor claims?
A: Defenses to creditor claims include: (1) Statute of limitations (claim accrued before death; creditor claim period for presentation to estate); (2) Claim lacks documentation or proof; (3) Debt was discharged (bankruptcy, prior payment); (4) Claim is time-barred under state law; (5) Debt was not valid obligation of decedent; (6) Claim is barred by defenses available to decedent (fraud, failure of consideration, breach by creditor); (7) Claim is invalid under applicable law; (8) Procedural defects in claim presentation. Burden typically on claimant to prove validity and amount of claim. Personal representative must evaluate claims objectively and defend estate against invalid claims while paying valid ones.

SECTION 7: TAX IMPLICATIONS & ESTATE PLANNING
================================================================================

Q71: What is the federal estate tax and what is the current exemption?
A: Federal estate tax (IRC §2001 et seq.) is imposed on transfer of decedent's taxable estate. Current exemption: $13.61 million per person (2024), scheduled to sunset to approximately $7 million (inflation-adjusted) in 2026 unless extended. Tax rate: 40% on amounts exceeding exemption. Taxable estate includes: all property interests owned at death, certain lifetime transfers, life insurance proceeds, jointly owned property (portion attributable to decedent), property over which decedent held general power of appointment, and QTIP property from spouse. Deductions: marital deduction (unlimited for qualifying transfers to spouse), charitable deduction, debts, administration expenses, losses. Return (Form 706) due 9 months after death (6-month extension available).

Q72: What is the unlimited marital deduction and what qualifies?
A: IRC §2056 allows unlimited deduction for property passing to surviving spouse, eliminating estate tax on interspousal transfers. Requirements: (1) Property must pass to surviving spouse (outright or qualifying trust); (2) Included in decedent's gross estate; (3) Spouse must be U.S. citizen (QDOT required for non-citizen spouse); (4) Must not be terminable interest (exceptions: QTIP trust, estate trust, certain annuities, life estates with general power of appointment). QTIP (Qualified Terminable Interest Property) trust qualifies if: (a) spouse entitled to all income for life, payable at least annually; (b) no power in anyone to appoint property to non-spouse during spouse's life; (c) executor elects QTIP treatment on estate tax return. QTIP included in surviving spouse's estate.

Q73: What is portability and how does it work?
A: Portability (IRC §2010(c)) allows surviving spouse to use deceased spouse's unused estate tax exemption (DSUE - Deceased Spousal Unused Exclusion). Features: (1) Deceased spouse's estate must file Form 706 electing portability (even if no tax due); (2) Filing deadline: 9 months plus extension (5 years for certain late elections); (3) DSUE amount added to surviving spouse's exemption; (4) Used for surviving spouse's lifetime gifts and estate; (5) Surviving spouse can only carry forward exemption from most recently deceased spouse; (6) DSUE adjusted for inflation. Advantage: allows full use of both spouses' exemptions without trust planning. Still consider bypass trusts for state estate tax, creditor protection, and appreciation sheltering.

Q74: What is the generation-skipping transfer tax and how does it apply?
A: GST tax (IRC §2601-2664) is additional tax on transfers to persons two or more generations below transferor (grandchildren, great-grandchildren) to prevent avoidance of estate tax across generations. Current exemption: $13.61 million (2024), same as estate tax exemption. Tax rate: 40% (maximum estate tax rate). Three types: (1) Direct skip - outright transfer to skip person; (2) Taxable termination - trust interest terminates, property continues in trust for skip persons; (3) Taxable distribution - distribution from trust to skip person. Exemption allocated to transfers (automatic allocation rules or manual allocation). Dynasty trusts use GST exemption to create multi-generational wealth transfer. Planning requires careful allocation and trust design.

Q75: What is the basis step-up rule and how does it work?
A: IRC §1014 provides that property acquired from decedent receives basis equal to fair market value at date of death (or alternate valuation date if elected). This "step-up" eliminates income tax on appreciation during decedent's life. Features: (1) Applies to most estate property; (2) Both appreciation and depreciation recognized ("step-down" possible); (3) Community property in community property states: both halves receive step-up (not just decedent's half); (4) Lifetime gifts do not receive step-up (carryover basis under IRC §1015); (5) Certain property exceptions (IRD - income in respect of decedent - no step-up); (6) Step-up repealed for 2010 deaths unless estate elected into estate tax. Planning consideration: may be better to hold appreciated property until death rather than gift.

Q76: What is abatement and how does it apply when estate assets are insufficient?
A: Abatement determines order for reducing bequests when estate assets insufficient to pay debts and all bequests. Default abatement order (absent will specification): (1) Intestate property; (2) Residuary bequests; (3) General legacies (specific dollar amount, not from specific source); (4) Demonstrative legacies (dollar amount from specific source, general if source insufficient); (5) Specific bequests (particular property). Within each class, abatement is pro rata unless will provides otherwise. Will may specify different abatement order. If spouse entitled to elective share, property passing to spouse may be protected from abatement. Secured debts typically paid from encumbered property, not causing abatement.

Q77: What is income in respect of a decedent (IRD)?
A: IRD (IRC §691) is income earned by decedent but not included in final income tax return (not received before death). Common examples: deferred compensation, installment sale payments, accrued interest, traditional IRA/401(k) distributions, accounts receivable, renewal commissions. Tax treatment: (1) No step-up in basis; (2) Included in estate for estate tax purposes; (3) Taxable to recipient when received; (4) Recipient gets IRC §691(c) deduction for estate tax paid on IRD (avoids double taxation); (5) Character flows through to recipient. Planning: consider Roth conversions, lifetime distributions, charitable bequests of IRD (charity pays no income tax, estate gets deduction).

Q78: What are qualified disclaimers and what are the requirements?
A: Qualified disclaimer (IRC §2518) allows beneficiary to refuse inheritance without gift tax consequences. Requirements: (1) Irrevocable and unqualified refusal to accept interest; (2) Writing; (3) Received by personal representative or transferor within 9 months of later of: death or beneficiary reaching age 21; (4) Disclaimant has not accepted interest or its benefits; (5) Interest passes without direction by disclaimant to: (a) spouse or (b) person who would receive had disclaimant predeceased. Results: treated as if disclaimant predeceased. Uses: tax planning (pass to lower generation, qualify for marital deduction, utilize exemptions), avoid creditors (in some states), simplify administration. State law disclaimer rules may differ; federal tax rules control for tax purposes.

Q79: What is the annual gift tax exclusion and how is it used in estate planning?
A: IRC §2503(b) allows annual exclusion of $18,000 per donee (2024) for present interest gifts. Features: (1) Indexed for inflation; (2) Unlimited number of donees; (3) Per-donor (married couples can split gifts for $36,000 per donee); (4) No filing requirement if within exclusion; (5) Present interest required (immediate use and enjoyment); (6) Future interests don't qualify (except §2503(c) trusts for minors, Crummey trusts). Uses: reduce taxable estate, provide support to family, equalize inheritances among children, fund education (§529 allows 5-year front-loading), make charitable gifts. Gifts exceeding exclusion require Form 709 and use lifetime exemption or incur gift tax.

Q80: What is a grantor retained annuity trust (GRAT) and how does it work?
A: GRAT is irrevocable trust where grantor retains annuity for term of years, with remainder to beneficiaries. Tax treatment: (1) Gift tax on creation equals present value of remainder (actuarially determined); (2) Annuity designed to "zero out" gift (annuity value approximately equals contributed assets); (3) Appreciation above IRC §7520 rate passes tax-free to beneficiaries; (4) If grantor survives term, assets excluded from estate; (5) If grantor dies during term, portion included in estate (proportional to remaining term). Planning: short terms reduce mortality risk, use appreciating assets, rolling GRATs. Requires grantor survive term. Popular for business owners and volatile assets. "Zeroed-out" GRATs have no downside if appreciation doesn't materialize.

SECTION 8: SPECIAL PROBATE SITUATIONS
================================================================================

Q81: How are digital assets handled in estate administration?
A: Digital assets include: email accounts, social media, digital photos, cryptocurrencies, domain names, online accounts, digital files. Issues: (1) Access - passwords, encryption, terms of service restrictions; (2) Legal authority - Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, terms of service prohibitions, privacy laws; (3) Identification - decedent may not have disclosed accounts. Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (RUFADAA, adopted in most states) provides: personal representative has authority to access digital assets unless user prohibited in online tool or will; service providers must comply with authorized requests; three tiers of content access (full content, catalog information, no access). Planning: maintain password list, authorize access in will/trust, use online tools provided by platforms, consider digital asset clauses.

Q82: What special rules apply to simultaneous death situations?
A: When order of death cannot be determined: (1) Uniform Simultaneous Death Act (USDA) presumes each person predeceased the other for purposes of their own property; (2) Life insurance proceeds paid as if insured survived beneficiary; (3) Joint tenancy property distributed half to each estate; (4) UPC 120-hour survival rule (described in Q28) requires clear and convincing evidence of survival for 120 hours. Planning considerations: will/trust should include simultaneous death clause specifying different disposition if primary beneficiary doesn't survive by specified period. Common disaster provisions address scenarios where spouses die closely in time. Prevents property from passing through both estates and ensures disposition to more remote beneficiaries.

Q83: How are unmarried cohabitants treated under probate law?
A: Unmarried partners (including same-sex couples not married and opposite-sex couples) generally have no intestate inheritance rights. Issues: (1) No spouse protections (elective share, homestead, allowances) unless jurisdiction recognizes common law marriage or domestic partnership; (2) No automatic property rights; (3) Not "family" for wrongful death or other purposes in most states; (4) May have standing to contest will in some states. Planning essential: (1) Execute wills naming partner as beneficiary; (2) Use joint tenancy or beneficiary designations for specific assets; (3) Consider trust planning; (4) Durable power of attorney for healthcare and finances; (5) Domestic partnership registration where available. Intestacy results in property passing to decedent's blood relatives, not partner.

Q84: What is a pretermitted heir statute and when does it apply?
A: Pretermitted heir statutes (also called omitted heir/child statutes) protect children born or adopted after will execution. Typical provisions: (1) Child born/adopted after will execution receives intestate share unless: (a) will shows intent to disinherit, (b) testator had other children and left substantially all to surviving parent, or (c) child provided for outside will; (2) Share taken from residuary, or if insufficient, from all devisees pro rata; (3) Majority rule: applies only to children, not other descendants; (4) Some states have pretermitted spouse statutes (protect spouse married after will). Does not apply if: will executed after child's birth, will shows intent to disinherit all children, or trust/life insurance beneficiary designations name child. Solution: execute new will/codicil after child's birth.

Q85: How are claims for elective share calculated and satisfied?
A: Elective share statutes protect surviving spouse from disinheritance. Traditional: fixed fraction (typically 1/3) of probate estate. UPC approach: sliding scale based on marriage length (3%-50%), calculated on "augmented estate" including: probate assets, revocable trusts, joint tenancy, beneficiary designations, lifetime transfers, and spouse's own assets. Spouse must elect within time limit (typically 6-9 months). Share satisfied from: property passing to spouse under will/intestacy/non-probate transfers credited first; if insufficient, contribution from other beneficiaries (often pro rata). Elective share is amount spouse receives, not additional amount. Waivable by prenuptial/postnuptial agreement. Community property states use different system (spouse owns 1/2 of community property).

Q86: What happens to jointly owned property when one owner dies?
A: Depends on ownership form: (1) Joint tenancy with right of survivorship - decedent's interest automatically passes to surviving joint tenants by operation of law, avoids probate, included in decedent's estate for tax purposes (portion attributable to decedent's contribution or 50% if joint tenant spouse); (2) Tenancy by the entirety - same as joint tenancy but only between spouses, creditor protection features; (3) Tenancy in common - decedent's share passes through probate to heirs/beneficiaries, no survivorship; (4) Community property with right of survivorship - available in some community property states, combines community property tax treatment with survivorship. Characterization depends on deed/title language. Disputes arise regarding intent, contribution, and whether joint tenancy was intended as will substitute.

Q87: What special rules apply to estate administration for non-resident aliens?
A: Non-resident alien decedents face different rules: (1) Estate tax applies only to U.S.-situs property (real property, tangible personal property in U.S., stock of U.S. corporations); (2) Estate tax exemption only $60,000 (vs. $13.61M for citizens/residents); (3) No marital deduction unless spouse is U.S. citizen (QDOT required); (4) Treaty benefits may apply; (5) Ancillary administration required in U.S. for U.S. property; (6) Domiciliary administration in home country. Planning: reduce U.S.-situs assets, use foreign corporations to hold U.S. property, life insurance planning, treaty planning, QDOT for non-citizen surviving spouse. Complex choice of law issues regarding validity and construction of will.

Q88: How are retirement accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s) handled in estate administration?
A: Retirement accounts pass by beneficiary designation, avoiding probate unless estate named beneficiary. Key issues: (1) Beneficiary designation controls regardless of will; (2) Required minimum distributions (RMDs) continue; (3) Spousal beneficiaries have special options (treat as own IRA, spousal rollover); (4) Non-spousal beneficiaries subject to 10-year distribution rule (SECURE Act 2.0 for deaths after 2019); (5) Eligible designated beneficiaries (surviving spouse, minor children, disabled/chronically ill, certain trusts, beneficiaries <10 years younger) can stretch distributions over life expectancy; (6) No step-up in basis (IRD property); (7) Estate as beneficiary requires distribution within 5 years. Planning: update beneficiary designations, consider conduit or accumulation trusts as beneficiaries, evaluate Roth conversions.

Q89: What are the special considerations for estate administration involving minor beneficiaries?
A: Minors cannot legally receive substantial property directly. Options and considerations: (1) Testamentary trust - best option for significant assets, trustee manages until specified age, can include support and education provisions; (2) Custodial account (UTMA/UGMA) - simpler, distributes at age 21 (UTMA) or 18 (UGMA); (3) Blocked account - court-supervised account requiring court approval for withdrawals; (4) Guardianship of estate - court-appointed guardian manages property, requires annual accountings and court approval for expenditures; (5) Minor's compromise - court approval required for settlements involving minors. Personal representative cannot distribute directly to minor. Court approval typically required for distributions exceeding minimal amounts. Guardian of person ≠ guardian of estate.

Q90: How is a decedent's business interest handled in probate?
A: Business interests require special attention: (1) Valuation - professional appraisal required, discount for minority interest and lack of marketability may apply; (2) Management - personal representative has limited authority to continue business (typically 4-6 months without court approval); (3) Buy-sell agreements - may require or permit sale to co-owners, must be followed, valuation formula typically specified; (4) Entity type matters - sole proprietorship winds down or continues briefly, partnership dissolves or continues per agreement, corporation shares transfer subject to restrictions, LLC governed by operating agreement; (5) Going concern value vs. liquidation value; (6) Successor management and transition. Planning: buy-sell agreements funded with life insurance, succession planning, entity choice, key person insurance.

SECTION 9: ANCILLARY & MULTI-JURISDICTIONAL ISSUES
================================================================================

Q91: What is ancillary administration and when is it required?
A: Ancillary administration is a secondary probate proceeding in a state other than the decedent's domicile state. Required when: (1) Decedent owned real property in non-domicile state; (2) Some states require ancillary for tangible personal property located in state. Process: (1) Domiciliary representative petitions for appointment as ancillary representative; (2) Abbreviated process compared to primary administration; (3) Authenticated copy of domiciliary letters and will; (4) Notice to creditors in ancillary state; (5) Distribution coordinated with domiciliary estate. Avoid through: revocable trust, joint tenancy, transfer-on-death deeds (available in some states), entity ownership (LLC/corporation).

Q92: What choice of law rules apply to wills and estates?
A: Complex choice of law rules apply: (1) Real property - law of situs (location of property) governs validity, interpretation, and succession; (2) Personal property - law of decedent's domicile at death governs succession; (3) Will execution validity - valid if complies with law of: place of execution, testator's domicile at execution, testator's domicile at death, or location of property (for real property); (4) Will construction/interpretation - often law of testator's domicile at death; (5) Spousal rights - law of domicile at death (majority) or domicile during marriage (minority); (6) Some states apply "most significant relationship" test. UPC §2-602 validates will if complies with any of several jurisdictions' laws. Results in strategic planning opportunities and potential conflicts.

Q93: How do community property and common law property systems interact in estate administration?
A: Conflicts arise when decedent or spouse move between community and common law states. Issues: (1) Quasi-community property - property acquired in common law state that would have been community property if acquired in current community property state; treated as community property for some purposes; (2) Property characterization - determined by law of domicile when acquired; (3) Transmutation - may change character if moved between states; (4) Elective share in common law states may not reach community property; (5) Community property retains character after move to common law state unless transmuted; (6) Tax treatment - community property receives full step-up in basis for both halves. Planning: characterization agreements, transmutation agreements where appropriate, consider elective share implications.

Q94: What is the Uniform Transfer on Death Security Registration Act?
A: UTOD allows securities to be registered in transfer-on-death (TOD) form, passing to designated beneficiary at death without probate. Features: (1) Adopted in all states; (2) Registration form: "[Owner] TOD [Beneficiary]"; (3) Owner retains full ownership rights during life; (4) Revocable until death; (5) Multiple or contingent beneficiaries permitted; (6) Beneficiary has no rights until death; (7) No creditor protection during life; (8) Subject to elective share in some states; (9) Estate tax inclusion. Parallel: TOD deeds for real property (available in approximately 30 states). Advantages: probate avoidance, simple, no loss of control during life. Disadvantages: no incapacity planning, potential unintended consequences, creditor exposure.

Q95: How are foreign wills handled in U.S. probate?
A: Foreign wills may be probated in U.S. for U.S. assets. Process: (1) Determine will validity under choice of law rules (see Q92); (2) Authenticate will (may require consularization or apostille); (3) Translation required if not in English; (4) File in state where assets located; (5) Comply with U.S. notice requirements; (6) Foreign personal representative may need to be appointed as ancillary representative; (7) Court determines foreign will validity and effect; (8) Potential conflicts between foreign law and U.S. law (forced heirship, community property); (9) Foreign probate may be recognized through exemplification/authentication. U.S. will executed for U.S. assets while foreign will covers foreign assets is common planning technique.

Q96: What is the Uniform Probate Code and what is its status of adoption?
A: UPC is a comprehensive model probate statute drafted by Uniform Law Commission, first promulgated in 1969, last revised 2019. Covers: intestacy, wills, probate administration, protection proceedings, non-probate transfers, trusts. Key features: (1) Simplified informal probate; (2) Extended survival period; (3) Per capita at each generation; (4) Augmented estate elective share; (5) Harmless error rule for wills; (6) Reformation for mistake. Adoption: approximately 18 states have adopted UPC substantially (varying degrees of modification). Many other states have adopted specific provisions. Not uniform across states; each enacting state modifies provisions. Represents modern trend but far from universal adoption.

Q97: What are the Hague Conventions relevant to international estates?
A: Several Hague Conventions affect international estate administration: (1) 1961 Convention Abolishing the Requirement of Legalisation for Foreign Public Documents (Apostille Convention) - simplifies authentication of foreign documents through apostille; widely adopted; (2) 1989 Hague Convention on the Law Applicable to Succession to the Estates of Deceased Persons - determines choice of law for succession; U.S. not a party; (3) 1973 Convention on the International Administration of the Estates of Deceased Persons - facilitates international estate administration; limited adoption. Issues: inconsistent adoption, varying reservations, interpretation differences. U.S. reliance primarily on domestic choice of law rules and bilateral treaties.

Q98: How do prenuptial and postnuptial agreements affect estate administration?
A: Marital agreements can waive or modify statutory rights: (1) Elective share waiver - most common, allows spouse to disinherit; (2) Intestate share waiver; (3) Homestead, exempt property, and allowance rights; (4) Community property rights waiver or characterization; (5) Rights in retirement accounts (requires qualified waiver for ERISA plans). Validity requirements: (1) Writing; (2) Voluntary - no duress; (3) Full disclosure of assets and obligations; (4) Fair and reasonable (timing varies by state - execution, enforcement, or both); (5) Independent counsel advisable; (6) Postnuptial requires consideration in some states. Enforcement issues arise regarding: adequacy of disclosure, pressure/duress, unconscionability, changed circumstances. Court scrutinizes agreements, especially prenuptial agreements executed close to wedding.

Q99: What is the Uniform Trust Code and how does it affect estate planning?
A: UTC is comprehensive model trust statute adopted in approximately 36 states. Key provisions: (1) Default rules (apply unless trust provides otherwise); (2) Trust creation, validity, modification, termination rules; (3) Trustee duties, powers, liability; (4) Trust reformation and modification (changed circumstances, uneconomical trusts); (5) Nonjudicial settlement agreements; (6) Virtual representation; (7) Trust merger and division; (8) Trustee resignation and removal; (9) Prudent investor rule incorporation. Impact: increased flexibility, statutory framework for common situations, trust decanting authority in some states' UTC adoptions, beneficiary rights clarification. Non-uniform adoption means provisions vary by state. Provides reliable default rules, reducing need for extensive drafting.

Q100: What constitutional issues arise in probate and estate administration?
A: Key constitutional issues: (1) Due process - adequate notice to interested parties, opportunity to be heard (Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.); (2) Equal protection - gender distinctions in intestacy laws (struck down), non-marital children discrimination (limitations on differential treatment), adopted children equal treatment required; (3) Takings - restrictions on testamentary freedom (generally permitted), forced heirship and elective share (constitutional); (4) Full faith and credit - recognition of foreign state judgments and proceedings; (5) Conflicts with federal law - supremacy clause (ERISA preemption of state beneficiary designation rules, federal tax law supremacy); (6) First Amendment - charitable trust restrictions, religious conditions in wills (generally permitted absent trust administration requiring illegal discrimination). Dead hand control limited by public policy but generally upheld.

Q101: What is standing to contest a will?
A: Standing requires: (1) "Interested person" - one who would benefit from successful contest (intestate heir, beneficiary under prior will, creditor); (2) Pecuniary interest that would be adversely affected by will probate; (3) Current beneficiary under will being probated generally lacks standing to contest (already receiving benefit). Requirements vary by state. Non-beneficiary family members typically lack standing if they would not inherit under intestacy. Potential beneficiaries under invalid prior will have standing. Creditors may have standing if will affects their claims. Issue may arise regarding: adequate disclosure of interest, capacity to sue, and real party in interest.

Q102: What is the no-contest clause and how is it enforced?
A: No-contest (in terrorem) clause penalizes beneficiaries who contest the will, typically forfeiting their bequest. Enforceability varies: (1) Majority rule - enforces unless contest brought in good faith and probable cause existed; (2) Strict enforcement states - enforces regardless of good faith or probable cause; (3) UPC approach - enforces only if no probable cause for contest; (4) Some states refuse enforcement or limit enforcement. Determines what constitutes "contest": direct will challenge typically covered, discovery/investigation may not be, construction/interpretation proceedings usually not covered, will inspection generally permitted. Clause must be clear and specific. Ineffective if entire estate would pass to contestant under intestacy. Strategic drafting includes nominal bequest to potential contestants.

Q103: What is the burden of proof in will contests?
A: Burden allocation varies by ground: (1) Will proponent burden - prove proper execution (testator capacity, formalities compliance, absence of fraud/undue influence) by preponderance (some states require clear and convincing evidence for lost wills); (2) Contestant burden - prove lack of capacity, undue influence, fraud, or duress by preponderance (some states clear and convincing); (3) Self-proving affidavit shifts burden - creates presumption of proper execution, contestant must rebut; (4) Confidential relationship + suspicious circumstances may shift burden to proponent to prove no undue influence. Evidence includes: witness testimony, medical records, circumstances surrounding execution, attorney drafting testimony, earlier wills, natural bounty analysis, opportunity for influence. Jury trial available in some states.

Q104: What is a lost will and how is it probated?
A: Lost will is will known to have been executed but cannot be located at death. Legal presumptions: (1) If last seen in testator's possession/control and not found, presumed revoked by physical act; (2) Presumption rebuttable by evidence of non-revocatory loss (accident, theft, safekeeping). Probate requirements: (1) Prove proper execution; (2) Prove non-revocation (overcome presumption); (3) Prove contents with sufficient certainty (copy, attorney notes, witness testimony); (4) Higher burden of proof (clear and convincing evidence in many states). Some states prohibit lost will probate. If admitted, will terms must be established. Duplicate originals help avoid lost will issues. Electronic wills raise new issues regarding loss and retention.

Q105: What is tortious interference with expectancy of inheritance?
A: Common law tort allowing recovery for interference with expected inheritance. Elements: (1) Expectancy of inheritance (reasonable, based on relationship and prior expressions); (2) Intentional interference by defendant (fraud, undue influence, destruction of will); (3) Causation; (4) Damages. Separate from will contest - brought as civil action, not probate proceeding. Advantages over will contest: (1) Not bound by probate time limits; (2) Jury trial available; (3) Punitive damages possible; (4) Discovery may be broader. Defendants often include: caregivers, new spouse, persons with access to testator. Requires proof actual inheritance would have occurred but for interference. Some states do not recognize tort.

Q106: What defenses are available in will contests?
A: Common defenses include: (1) Laches - unreasonable delay in bringing contest (applies even if within statute of limitations); (2) Estoppel - contestant's prior conduct inconsistent with contest; (3) Ratification - contestant's acceptance of benefits under will; (4) Unclean hands - contestant's misconduct; (5) Statute of limitations - typically short (3-6 months from admission to probate in most states); (6) No-contest clause forfeiture; (7) Lack of standing; (8) Failure to meet burden of proof; (9) Lack of personal jurisdiction over necessary parties. Procedural defenses include improper service, failure to join necessary parties, and failure to state claim. Strategic considerations include settlement, mediation, and partial contest.

Q107: What is the doctrine of dependent relative revocation in contested estates?
A: DRR (described in Q17) frequently arises in will contests. Applications: (1) Testator revokes will believing new will is valid; new will fails due to lack of capacity, undue influence, or formality defect; court may revive prior will under DRR if closer to testator's intent than intestacy; (2) Court weighs similarity between revoked will and failed new will - DRR more likely if instruments similar; (3) Burden on party seeking DRR application; (4) Some states reject DRR entirely. Related: revival doctrine (revoked will revived by revoking later will - some states automatic, others require intent, some prohibit). Complex interaction between DRR, revival, and holographic codicils or markings on wills.

Q108: How are attorney fees handled in will contests?
A: Fee shifting varies by jurisdiction and circumstances: (1) American rule default - each party pays own fees; (2) Exceptions allowing fee recovery: (a) prevailing party in many states if contest benefits estate or resolves reasonable doubt; (b) estate pays fees if contest in good faith on probable cause (some states); (c) losing contestant may be surcharged fees if acted in bad faith; (d) will may provide for fee payment; (3) Settlement agreements typically address fees; (4) Personal representative fees paid from estate for defending will (if acting reasonably). Attorney representing personal representative typically paid from estate. Attorney for contestant paid by contestant unless fee-shifting applies. Fee petitions require documentation and court approval. Contingency fees common for contestants.

Q109: What is the role of mediation in probate disputes?
A: Mediation increasingly used for estate disputes: (1) Court-ordered or voluntary mediation programs in many jurisdictions; (2) Advantages: confidentiality, preserves family relationships, creative solutions beyond legal remedies, cost savings, faster resolution, party control; (3) Suitable disputes: will contests, trust disputes, accounting objections, fee disputes, interpretation questions; (4) Mediator typically experienced in probate/estate law; (5) Settlement agreements enforceable as contracts and typically approved by probate court; (6) May be required before trial in some jurisdictions. Limitations: power imbalances between parties, unrepresented parties, complex tax issues requiring specialist input. Success rate high for good-faith participants. Alternative: arbitration (binding resolution by neutral arbiter).

Q110: What ethical issues arise for attorneys in estate administration?
A: Key ethical issues: (1) Who is the client - decedent's intent vs. personal representative vs. beneficiaries; (2) Conflicts of interest - representing multiple beneficiaries, personal representative who is also beneficiary, prior representation of decedent; (3) Confidentiality - decedent's confidences, attorney-client privilege survival, disclosure to beneficiaries; (4) Safekeeping wills - duties regarding storage, notification, delivery; (5) Drafting attorney as witness to will execution (prohibited in some states); (6) Drafting attorney as beneficiary (presumed undue influence, voidable in many states); (7) Lawyer as fiduciary - serving as executor/trustee while providing legal services. Model Rules of Professional Conduct provide guidance but state variations exist. Best practices: clear engagement letters, conflict waivers, avoid beneficial interests, document decision-making.
