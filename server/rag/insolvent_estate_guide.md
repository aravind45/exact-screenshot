# Insolvent Estate Guidance for Executors

When an estate's total debts exceed its total assets, it is considered legally "insolvent." As an executor or administrator, handling an insolvent estate is extremely high-risk. If you pay the wrong creditor or distribute assets to heirs when there are outstanding debts, you can be held personally liable for the unpaid debts.

Follow these critical instructions when dealing with an insolvent estate:

## 1. Stop All Distributions Immediately
Do not distribute any property, cash, or heirlooms to beneficiaries or heirs. Beneficiaries receive absolute bottom priority in an insolvent estate. In most cases, they will receive nothing. 

## 2. Stop All Creditor Payments
Do not pay any credit card bills, personal loans, or medical bills out of the estate account (or out of your own pocket). You must wait until the creditor claim period expires and you have a complete picture of all debts.

## 3. Understand the Statutory Priority of Claims
State law dictates the exact order in which debts must be paid. If you do not have enough money to pay everyone in a particular class (tier), the creditors in that class receive a pro-rata (proportional) share, and all lower classes receive nothing.

While exact laws vary by state, the general priority order is:
1. **Administration Expenses**: Court fees, attorney fees, executor commissions, and costs to preserve estate assets (e.g., keeping the lights on at the decedent's house).
2. **Funeral & Burial Expenses**: Reasonable costs for the funeral and burial (often capped by statute).
3. **Federal Taxes & Debts**: Any taxes owed to the IRS. *Critical Note: The federal government must always be paid before general creditors.*
4. **State Taxes & Debts**: State income tax, state medical recovery programs (Medicaid/Medi-Cal recovery), etc.
5. **Secured Debts**: Mortgages or car loans, but only up to the value of the collateral.
6. **Judgments**: Court judgments entered against the decedent before death.
7. **Unsecured General Debts**: Credit cards, personal loans, utility bills, etc.

## 4. Obtain Tax Clearances
Wait to receive official tax clearances (e.g., an IRS estate closing letter or transcript, and a state tax clearance certificate) before finalizing any payments to lower-tier creditors.

## 5. Formal Legal Filings
You must usually file a formal "Declaration of Insolvency" or a "Petition to Declare Estate Insolvent" with the probate court. The court will then officially order how the remaining assets should be divided among the creditors.
